Asthma

Phase 1 and 2

Asthma is a chronic, heterogeneous disease of the lower airways. Ongoing inflammation and airway hyperreactivity cause cough, wheeze, breathlessness and chest tightness that vary over time. Worldwide, an estimated 260 million people are affected.

When inflammation is undertreated, lung function can decline, control worsens, exacerbation risk rises and the airways can remodel. Control has two linked domains: current symptom control and future risk. A patient can have few symptoms and still carry a high risk of exacerbation, which is why assessment covers both.

Nature
Chronic airway inflammation with variable airflow limitation
Typical symptoms
Cough, wheeze, breathlessness, chest tightness

Assessing control

GINA symptom-control questions (past 4 weeks). Count the “yes” answers: none is well controlled, 1–2 partly controlled, 3–4 uncontrolled.

  • Daytime asthma symptoms more than twice a week?
  • Any night waking due to asthma?
  • Reliever needed for symptoms more than twice a week?
  • Any activity limitation due to asthma?

Asthma Control Test (ACT). Five items, scored 5 to 25. A score of 20 or more is well controlled, 16–19 not well controlled and 15 or less very poorly controlled. A change of 3 points is considered clinically meaningful.

Perception gap (links to C5). Patients often underestimate symptoms and overestimate control. In one Asia-Pacific survey reported in the MERIT paper, 56% of Malaysian respondents described their asthma as controlled, while measured control was as low as 6%.
Mild asthma is not always mild. In a Malaysian primary care cohort, about half of patients on GINA Step 1–2 treatment still had uncontrolled or partly controlled asthma, and around 46% had at least one severe exacerbation in the past year.

GINA stepwise treatment for adults and adolescents

GINA 2025 and 2026 keep two tracks. Track 1 (preferred) uses ICS-formoterol as the reliever. Track 2 is the alternative, with a separate reliever. Seretide sits in Track 2.

What is new in GINA 2026 (published 5 May 2026)

  • Four new flowcharts for assessing and treating acute asthma in primary care and acute care settings, with ICS-formoterol added as an option and a lower oxygen threshold (supplemental oxygen only if saturation is below 92%).
  • Track 2 gains ICS-SABA as an anti-inflammatory reliever at Step 1.
  • Step 5 guidance updated, including a budesonide-formoterol-glycopyrronium triple option.
  • New biologic options: depemokimab and a generic anti-IgE.
  • A list of red flags for fatal or near-fatal asthma, and a push to minimise oral corticosteroid use.
  • New assessment tools such as the Chronic Airways Assessment Test (CAAT), and inhaled doses now mostly expressed as delivered doses.
Reliever overuse. GINA 2025 notes that dispensing three or more SABA canisters a year is linked to a higher risk of emergency visits or hospitalisation, and twelve or more to a higher risk of death. In Track 2, check that patients are actually taking their maintenance ICS-containing therapy.

Seretide (salmeterol/fluticasone propionate)

ICS/LABA

Seretide combines two drugs with different modes of action in one inhaler: fluticasone propionate (FP), an inhaled corticosteroid that reduces airway inflammation, and salmeterol, a long-acting β2-agonist (LABA) that relaxes airway smooth muscle for at least 12 hours. It is taken regularly, usually twice daily, and is not a reliever.

Track 1 versus Track 2 in content. GINA prefers Track 1 (ICS-formoterol reliever). Patients whose maintenance ICS-LABA contains a non-formoterol LABA, such as salmeterol, should not use ICS-formoterol as their reliever. Any content that mentions MART should keep this distinction clear.

Mechanism and pharmacology

  • Salmeterol is a selective long-acting β2-agonist (about 12 hours) with a long side chain that binds the receptor’s exo-site. It also inhibits release of mast-cell mediators and attenuates bronchial hyper-responsiveness.
  • Fluticasone propionate has a potent local glucocorticoid anti-inflammatory action in the lungs, reducing symptoms and exacerbations without the effects seen with systemic steroids.
  • Fluticasone has very low oral bioavailability (under 1%), moderately high plasma protein binding (about 91%), high clearance and a terminal half-life of about 8 hours.
  • Absolute FP bioavailability in healthy adults is 5.3% for the Seretide Evohaler and 5.5% for the Seretide Accuhaler.

Safety points from the label

  • LABA warning. LABAs alone increase the risk of asthma-related death. Seretide should be prescribed for patients not adequately controlled on an inhaled corticosteroid, or whose severity clearly needs both. Once control is achieved, review regularly and step down if possible.
  • Do not stop abruptly. Titrate down under physician supervision, to the lowest dose that keeps control.
  • Common effects: headache (very common); oral candidiasis, hoarseness, muscle cramps and joint pain (common). Systemic corticosteroid effects are possible with high doses over long periods.
  • Interactions: avoid fluticasone with ritonavir; take care with strong CYP3A4 inhibitors such as ketoconazole; avoid beta-blockers unless there is a compelling reason.
  • Caution in thyrotoxicosis, pre-existing cardiovascular disease and patients prone to low potassium.

Clinical evidence

  • GOAL — 12-month study in 3,416 patients comparing Seretide with ICS alone, stepping treatment up every 12 weeks toward guideline-defined control. Well-controlled asthma was reached by 71% versus 59%, and total control by 41% versus 28%. Exacerbation rate was 29% lower with Seretide. In patients reaching control, 61% reported minimal or no quality-of-life impairment (8% at baseline). Asthma control and quality of life
  • MERIT — real-world, retrospective study across ten Malaysian sites in 120 adults with uncontrolled asthma (ACT below 20) stepped up to regular Seretide from as-needed budesonide/formoterol (63%) or ICS whenever SABA was taken (37%). At a mean of 5.8 months, 91.7% were responders (ACT 20 or more, or a gain of 3 or more); mean ACT rose from 14.4 to 21.7. Moderate exacerbations fell from 50.8% to 29.2%, no patient reported a severe exacerbation at follow-up, and no adverse drug reactions were recorded. Limits: no comparator, short follow-up, retrospective, GSK-funded. Step-up in the real world
  • AUSTRI — 26-week safety study in 11,679 adults and adolescents with moderate-to-severe asthma. Salmeterol-FP was non-inferior to FP alone for serious asthma-related events (0.6% in each arm). Exacerbations occurred in 8% versus 10% (hazard ratio 0.79). Safety and exacerbations
  • VESTRI — the paediatric counterpart in 6,208 children aged 4–11. Non-inferiority for serious events was met (0.9% versus 0.7%); the exacerbation reduction (9% versus 10%) was not statistically significant. Paediatric safety
  • SMART (salmeterol alone) — 28-week US study of salmeterol versus placebo added to usual therapy: 13 versus 3 asthma-related deaths among about 13,000 patients per arm. It was not designed to test the effect of concurrent ICS, and it underpins the LABA class warning. Safety context
  • Lower-ICS-dose studies — two studies showed better lung function, more symptom-free days and less rescue use than ICS alone at a 60% lower ICS dose, with airway inflammation on biopsy and lavage staying controlled. ICS-sparing effect
