The Servier project produces medical content for healthcare professionals (HCPs) in India on haemorrhoidal disease (HD) and, to a smaller extent, chronic venous disease (CVD), with Daflon (micronized purified flavonoid fraction, MPFF) as the Servier product in focus. Content is published on MedSynapse through the Venosphere CxCenter and is built on Content Strategy V3.1.

Phase 1 is a 9-month programme of 18 contents plus one pre-campaign survey. Contents 1 to 10 have been published; Contents 11 to 18 are still to come.

Haemorrhoidal disease (HD)

Primary focus

HD is a common anorectal disorder in which the natural anal cushions expand and are displaced distally, causing symptoms. It is classed as internal (above the dentate line) or external (below it). Internal HD is graded by degree of prolapse. The ACRSI practice guidelines (2016) propose the grading used in this project; they are written for general practitioners, general surgeons, colorectal surgeons and gastrointestinal surgeons in India.

Lifetime occurrence
Up to 75% of people at some point
Age
More than half of people over 50 have had haemorrhoidal problems
Sex
Men seek treatment more often; pregnancy makes women more prone to symptomatic HD
Typical symptoms
Bleeding, prolapse, pain, discomfort, pruritus, discharge
Under-reported and under-treated. The Project Overview deck notes that stigma and self-management keep many patients from care, with effects on quality of life, discomfort, bleeding and work productivity. This supports the early-diagnosis angle of Phase 1 and the Phase 2 theme on stigma and delayed presentation.

Grading of internal HD (ACRSI 2016, as shown in the project deck)

GradeDescriptionUsual starting point
Grade IBleeding, no prolapseLifestyle measures and MPFF; office procedure if persistent
Grade IIProlapse that reduces spontaneouslyLifestyle measures and MPFF; office procedures (rubber band ligation, sclerotherapy, infrared coagulation)
Grade IIIProlapse needing manual reductionMPFF for selected or minor cases; office procedures for selected cases; surgery for others
Grade IVIrreducible prolapseSurgical management, with MPFF as an adjunct

The “usual starting point” column simplifies the management slides in the project deck. It is not a treatment algorithm.

Management approach (project deck, citing ACRSI 2016)

  • Dietary modification with more fibre and adequate fluid as first-line treatment.
  • Manage predominant constipation with polyethylene glycol, lactulose or bulking agents.
  • First-line for Grade I–II, and for selected or minor Grade III.
  • Acutely bleeding HD can be treated effectively.
  • Adjunct to office-based and surgical procedures.
  • The deck states that other venoactive drugs have lower evidence support (a comparative statement; see Claims watch-outs).
Acute HD attack (MPFF 1000 mg)3 tablets a day for 4 days, then 2 tablets a day for 3 days
Maintenance (MPFF 1000 mg)1 tablet once daily; the deck says a minimum of 2 months or more at physician’s discretion
  • Rubber band ligation.
  • Injection sclerotherapy.
  • Infrared coagulation.
  • Stapled haemorrhoidopexy (Grade III–IV): more effective for pain control.
  • Haemorrhoidectomy (Grade III–IV).
  • DGHAL / THD (Grade II–IV): recurrence in Grade III–IV remains a limitation; combining DGHAL with anopexy or mucopexy may reduce recurrence and extend its use to advanced disease.
  • MPFF can be used as an adjunct to both office-based procedures and surgery.

How MPFF acts in HD (project deck, adapted from Porwal 2025)

  • Venous tone and microcirculation: increases venous tone and tightens endothelial junctions.
  • Capillary permeability: inhibits plasma leakage and reduces endothelial permeability.
  • Leukocyte–endothelial adhesion: reduces leukocyte adhesion and limits release of inflammatory mediators.
  • Lymphatic drainage: improves lymphatic clearance and relieves tissue oedema.

Chronic venous disease (CVD)

Secondary focus

CVD covers long-standing abnormalities of the venous system, mainly of the lower limbs, from heavy legs and oedema to skin changes and ulcers. The project deck says CVD is estimated to affect over half the population and that men seek treatment for it more often than women, although pregnancy makes women more prone to symptomatic disease. It also notes that haemorrhoids and CVD share risk factors, which underpins the dual-burden case study (C17).

Daflon strength for CVD
Daflon 500 mg only (the 1000 mg label covers haemorrhoids only)
CVD indications (500 mg label)
Chronic organic and functional venous insufficiency of the lower limbs (heavy legs, pain, night cramps, oedema); varicose veins; sequelae of phlebitis
Label dose
2 tablets a day (one at lunch, one at dinner)
Label duration
3 months in early CVD, 6 months in advanced stages
Verify the 4% figure. The deck says about 4% of adults over 65 have CVD, next to “over 50% of the population”. These two figures sit uneasily together. Check the source and definition (for example CEAP class) before using either in content.

VEIN STEP — the study behind C16

DesignProspective, observational, international, real life
Analysis set6,084 patients, 78% women, nine countries
Mean age / BMI50.6 years / 28.0 kg/m²
Follow-upWeeks 2 and 4 (optional week 8)
  • Most common CEAP classes: C1 23.0%, C2 31.6%, C3 30.7%.
  • Treatment: oral venoactive drugs in 95.8% (MPFF 75.5%, diosmin 18.8%), compression 52.0%, topicals 31.5%.
  • Global symptom improvement in 89% at 2 weeks and 96% at 4 weeks. Pain, leg heaviness, cramps and swelling improved in 82%, 71%, 45.5% and 46%.
  • MPFF-based therapy was linked to the greatest reduction in symptoms and signs, and the greatest CIVIQ-14 improvement.
  • Post-hoc type 2 diabetes subanalysis (702 of 6,084 patients): MPFF reduced global symptom intensity more than diosmin (−2.7 vs −2.0, p=0.004).

The Phase 1 strategy describes VEIN STEP as “randomised controlled trial (RCT) data”. The registry record and the publication both describe it as an observational study with no randomised comparator. Reword the C16 brief before development.

Daflon (micronized purified flavonoid fraction)

Venoactive · C05CA53

Daflon is a micronized purified flavonoid fraction: 90% diosmin and 10% flavonoids expressed as hesperidin, in micronized form to improve bioavailability. It is described as a vasoprotective and capillary-stabilising agent. The project deck says it was first introduced 37 years ago and is available as 500 mg and 1000 mg tablets. Servier India Private Limited manufactures it in India under licence from Les Laboratoires Servier, France.

Source and version. Details below come from the Daflon 500 mg and 1000 mg prescribing information (both Ver 4.0, April 2022) supplied with the project. Check for a newer approved label before any claim is used.

Strengths at a glance

Composition per tabletMPFF 500 mg: diosmin 450 mg, hesperidin 50 mg
PackBox of 5 × 2 × 10 tablets
Usual dose2 tablets a day, one at lunch and one at dinner
Acute haemorrhoidal attack6 tablets a day for 4 days, then 4 a day for 3 days

Indications

  • Phlebology: chronic organic and functional venous insufficiency of the lower limbs (heavy legs, pain, night cramps, oedema), varicose veins, sequelae of phlebitis.
  • Proctology: haemorrhoids and acute haemorrhoidal attacks.
  • Gynaecology: clinical manifestations of capillary fragility: dysfunctional uterine bleeding (DUB) and intrauterine contraceptive device (IUCD) induced bleeding.

Other doses. Maintenance after an acute attack is 2 tablets a day for as long as the physician decides. CVD: 2 tablets a day for 3 months (early) or 6 months (advanced). IUCD bleeding: 2 tablets a day for 3 consecutive cycles. DUB: 2 tablets a day from 5 days before expected menstruation to the end of bleeding, for 3 consecutive cycles.

Composition per tabletMPFF 1000 mg: diosmin 900 mg, hesperidin 100 mg
PackBox of 6 blisters × 10 tablets (not all pack sizes may be marketed)
Haemorrhoids, maintenance1 tablet daily, duration at physician’s discretion
Acute haemorrhoidal attack3 tablets a day for 4 days, then 2 a day for 3 days

Indication: treatment of haemorrhoids and acute haemorrhoidal attacks only. There is no venous or gynaecological indication on this label.

Same total daily MPFF, two strengths. The acute-attack regimen is 3,000 mg a day for 4 days then 2,000 mg a day for 3 days on the 1000 mg label, which is the same as 6 then 4 tablets of the 500 mg. Content should name the strength it refers to.

Mechanism and pharmacology

  • Dual action on venous return: at vein and venule level it increases parietal tone and has an anti-stasis action; at microcirculation level it strengthens capillary resistance and normalises capillary permeability.
  • Venous tone: venous occlusion plethysmography showed a shorter venous emptying time. Dose–effect relationships were significant for capacitance, distensibility and emptying time, and the best dose–effect ratio was with 2 tablets (500 mg label).
  • Microcirculation: double-blind studies versus placebo showed higher capillary resistance in patients with capillary fragility.
  • Pharmacokinetics (carbon-14 diosmin): excretion mainly in faeces; urinary excretion averages 14% of the dose; elimination half-life 11 hours; highly metabolised (phenol acids in urine).
  • Preclinical: no special hazard in repeat-dose, genotoxicity or reproductive toxicity studies.

Safety points from the label

  • Contraindication: hypersensitivity to the active substance or any excipient.
  • Acute attack: treatment must be short-term and does not exclude treatment of other anal conditions. If symptoms do not settle quickly, do a proctological examination and review treatment.
  • Pregnancy: limited data; animal studies show no reproductive toxicity. As a precaution, the label says it is preferable to avoid Daflon during pregnancy.
  • Breast-feeding: not known whether it is excreted in milk; a risk to the infant cannot be excluded. Decide whether to stop breast-feeding or abstain from Daflon.
  • Interactions: no interaction studies have been done; no clinically relevant interaction has been reported from post-marketing experience.
  • Driving: no or negligible influence.
  • Paediatric: safety and efficacy under 18 years not established.
  • Adverse effects: common — diarrhoea, dyspepsia, nausea, vomiting; uncommon — colitis; rare — dizziness, headache, malaise, rash, pruritus, urticaria; unknown frequency — abdominal pain, face/lip/eyelid oedema, exceptionally Quincke’s oedema.
  • Overdose: limited experience; mainly gastrointestinal and skin events; treat symptoms.
  • Reporting: suspected adverse reactions via PvPI, ADR helpline 1800 180 3024.

Shelf life 36 months; no special storage conditions; excipients include gelatin, microcrystalline cellulose, talc, sodium starch glycolate, magnesium stearate, sodium lauryl sulphate and polyethylene glycol 6000.

Evidence and clinical trials

Phase 1 and 2

A starting list of sources for content teams, built from the project references, PubMed, ClinicalTrials.gov and the two Servier websites. It is a research aid, not a vetted reference pack. Confirm each source, and its wording, before it goes into content.

Guidelines and systematic reviews

  • ACRSI Practice Guidelines 2016 — Agarwal N et al. Executive summary, Indian J Surg 2017;79(1):58–61. Covers diagnosis and dietary, non-surgical and surgical management; written for GPs, general surgeons, colorectal and GI surgeons in India. DOI C4, C5, C10, C12
  • MPFF in HD: systematic review and meta-analysis — Sheikh P, Lohsiriwat V, Shelygin Y. Adv Ther 2020;37(6):2792–2812. 11 RCTs (13 articles) versus placebo or no treatment. Pooled analysis of four studies: bleeding OR 0.082 (95% CI 0.027–0.250), discharge/leakage OR 0.12, overall improvement OR 5.25 (patients) and 5.51 (investigators); pain tended to fall (OR 0.11, p=0.06). DOI C7, C8
  • MPFF after haemorrhoidectomy: meta-analysis — Fu H et al. Phytomedicine 2022;104:154244. 22 RCTs, 2,335 participants. MPFF improved clinical efficacy and reduced bleeding, pain and oedema scores after haemorrhoidectomy; dose and duration mattered. Small samples; no standard regimen could be set. DOI C5, C12
  • Recurrence and pharmacological treatment: systematic review — Lohsiriwat V et al. Adv Ther 2022;40(1):117–132. 44 publications. Recurrence 0–56.5% after surgery or phlebotonics, most studies 20% or less. MPFF improved main symptoms more than other pharmacological treatments. No real-world study of recurrence on systemic or topical treatment was found. DOI C9, C12, C14
  • MPFF for haemorrhoids: earlier meta-analysis (a more cautious view) — Aziz Z et al. Complement Ther Med 2018;39:49–55. 10 RCTs, 1,164 participants. Bleeding favoured MPFF (RR 1.46, p=0.008), but evidence was low quality and other outcomes were not significant. Useful to know before claiming broad benefit. DOI Balance check

IMPACT-HD, and the MPFF in HD review in the project reference list, are named in the Project Overview deck. We could not match them to PubMed records in this pass; take the full citations from the team’s reference pack.

These are registry entries, many with unknown or unreported results. They are leads, not evidence to cite.

Standing reminders

  • Superscript references throughout; no unreferenced efficacy or safety statements.
  • Don’t use outdated prescribing information; both PI files provided are April 2022 (Ver 4.0).
  • Don’t describe observational data as randomised or as proof of causation.
  • Don’t repeat points already covered in published C1–C10 (the deck asks for non-repetitive content).
  • Don’t write content only for surgeons or only for physicians; it must work for both General Medicine and General Surgery.
  • Don’t include patient-facing promotional material; the Servier site states promotional content cannot be shared with patients.
