Asthma: control first

Asthma control has two linked domains: current symptom control and future risk of adverse outcomes. A patient may have few symptoms but still carry significant future risk.

What is asthma control?

Asthma control is the extent to which asthma manifestations are reduced or removed by treatment. It reflects the interaction between underlying disease processes, genetic background, treatment, environment and psychosocial factors.

Domain 1: Current symptom control

Daytime symptoms, night waking, reliever need and activity limitation.

Domain 2: Future risk

Exacerbations, impaired lung function, airway remodelling and treatment-related adverse outcomes.

Remember: good symptom control does not necessarily mean low risk. Review lung function, exacerbation history, SABA use, Type 2 biomarkers, adherence, technique and comorbidities.

Clinical remission

On-treatment clinical remission is a sustained outcome assessed over at least 12 months, not simply a short period without symptoms.

Four required criteria

1. Sustained absence of asthma symptoms.
2. Lung-function optimisation and stabilisation.
3. Patient and clinician agreement regarding disease remission.
4. No systemic corticosteroid use for exacerbation treatment or long-term disease control.

Four core domains over ≥12 months

No systemic corticosteroid use.
Asthma control.
No exacerbations.
Stable or optimised lung function.
Complete remission adds: resolution of asthma-related inflammation and current negative bronchial hyperresponsiveness. These require objective assessment and should not be inferred from symptom improvement alone.

Asthma ICS/LABA strategy: MART versus conventional

Track 1: MART

The preferred 2025 GINA track at Steps 3–4 uses an appropriate low-to-medium-dose ICS/formoterol inhaler for both maintenance and relief.

Track 2: conventional

Regular maintenance ICS/LABA plus an as-needed SABA may be suitable when MART is not possible, or for stable patients with good adherence and no exacerbations in the previous year.

Brand clarification: Relvar/Breo and Seretide/Advair are conventional maintenance ICS/LABA combinations, not MART inhalers, because their LABAs are vilanterol or salmeterol rather than formoterol.

Personalise the choice

  • Assess efficacy, symptoms, exacerbation history, lung function, SABA use and Type 2 markers where relevant.
  • Consider BMI, smoking history, sex, beliefs, treatment goals and expectations.
  • Check device ability, inhaler technique, adherence and preference for one versus multiple inhalers.
  • If efficacy or adherence falls, reassess before changing; avoid abrupt unaccompanied switching from a well-used device.

COPD: understand the disease

COPD is a chronic, progressive respiratory disease characterised by persistent symptoms and airflow limitation, commonly associated with smoking or other long-term exposure to noxious particles and gases. It may include emphysema, chronic bronchitis features or both, with important systemic manifestations and comorbidities.

What to assess

  • Dyspnoea, cough, sputum, wheeze and activity limitation.
  • Airflow limitation and lung-function trajectory, including FEV1.
  • Exacerbation history, hospitalisations and treatment requirements.
  • Smoking, biomass fuel, occupational and outdoor air-pollution exposures.
  • Cardiovascular disease, diabetes, osteoporosis, lung cancer, depression and other comorbidities.

Core goals

  • Relieve symptoms and improve exercise capacity and health-related quality of life.
  • Reduce exacerbations, hospitalisations and future risk.
  • Support smoking cessation, vaccination, physical activity and self-management.
  • Slow functional decline where possible and manage advanced respiratory failure in selected patients.

COPD: remember the escalation

Think: open the airways first, combine bronchodilators when symptoms persist, then consider ICS-containing therapy for selected exacerbation-prone patients with features supporting ICS use.

COPD management beyond inhalers

Prevention and lifestyle

  • Smoking cessation is a central disease-modifying intervention.
  • Reduce exposure to biomass fuel, occupational dust, fumes and air pollution where possible.
  • Use recommended influenza, pneumococcal and other locally indicated vaccines.
  • Support physical activity, nutritional optimisation and self-management education.

Pulmonary rehabilitation

Assessment-led rehabilitation can include exercise training, education, breathing strategies, self-management and psychosocial support. It can improve exercise tolerance, dyspnoea and quality of life; ongoing activity helps maintain gains.

Advanced supportive care

  • LTOT may improve survival in selected patients with severe chronic resting hypoxaemia; it is not automatically indicated for moderate desaturation.
  • Home NIV may reduce hospitalisation and mortality in selected chronic hypercapnic patients.
  • Specialist assessment may be appropriate for lung-volume-reduction procedures or transplantation in carefully selected end-stage disease.

Adjunctive pharmacological options

  • Roflumilast may be considered for selected frequent exacerbators with chronic bronchitis phenotype; adverse effects and eligibility matter.
  • Long-term macrolide therapy may reduce exacerbations in selected patients, but antimicrobial resistance, cardiac and hearing risks require consideration.
  • Mucolytics may have a role in selected patients with troublesome sputum or frequent exacerbations.

One-minute escalation

  • Asthma: assess current symptoms and future risk → establish ICS-based control → step up to conventional ICS/LABA such as Relvar or Seretide when appropriate → consider Trelegy for eligible adults uncontrolled despite ICS/LABA → consider Nucala for eligible severe eosinophilic disease.
  • COPD: assess symptoms, airflow limitation, exacerbations and exposures → use long-acting bronchodilation such as Incruse or Serevent → use Anoro for dual LAMA/LABA bronchodilation when needed → consider Relvar/Breo or Trelegy for selected exacerbation-prone patients with an ICS indication → add rehabilitation, prevention and advanced support according to need.
  • Cross-cutting message: the best regimen is personalised. Check diagnosis, adherence, technique, device ability, patient preference, comorbidities, exacerbation risk, lung function and local guidance before escalating or changing treatment.
